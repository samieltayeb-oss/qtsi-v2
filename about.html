Executive Profile

Built through setbacks. Focused on what truly matters.

Manav Chadha didn’t set out to build a career in cybersecurity. He built it one problem at a time, and he is the first to say he learned most of it by getting things wrong before he got them right.

For more than 20 years he has worked across nearly every industry, on the ground from APAC and EMEA to North America. He has sat with operators on the factory floor and with executives in the boardroom, and the same lesson kept finding him in every market: technology only matters when it moves the business, and risk only gets managed when someone decides to own it.

The heart of that work today runs through QTSI. It is where he helps organizations build security programs the business actually respects, grounded in the frameworks that carry weight: the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, ISO 27001, and governance, risk, and compliance done properly rather than performed for a binder. Much of it starts with a threat and risk assessment, the unglamorous work of finding where an organization is genuinely exposed before deciding what to protect first. What most people never see on a resume is the requirements and stakeholder work woven through all of it, the workshops, the process mapping, and the patient job of translating what the business truly needs into something IT can build and security can defend.

He is honest about what he is not. He came up through enterprise architecture, not code. He has never been a developer, and he does not pretend to be. His contribution sits a level above the keyboard, in the place where technology, security, and business strategy have to become one clear decision, and he does his best to carry that conversation in either direction, to the board and to the teams who make the work real.

Along the way, the learning became the point. Manav has written two books. The first is a practical guide for business owners on growing in the digital age. The second, The AI Mandate, makes the case that AI governance belongs in the CEO’s office, not the IT room, and it comes straight from the field rather than the sidelines.

He is also building something. It is an early-stage AI governance platform that sits between an organization’s employees and the everyday AI tools they rely on, strips sensitive data before it ever reaches the vendor, and quietly generates the SOC 2 audit evidence most teams dread assembling. It is early, and he says so plainly. But the problem it solves is real, and most organizations are nowhere near ready for it.

None of that is the part he is proudest of. He has mentored and trained more than 500 people moving into IT and cybersecurity, but the number is not what stays with him. What stays with him is one person who emailed after failing three interviews to say that his feedback had changed how they showed up. That, to him, is the work most worth doing. It is why his mission now reaches beyond any single company: to help build cybersecurity awareness for a million people around the world, in language anyone can understand.

That same spirit reaches well beyond his work. He gives back to the profession that shaped him, volunteering with ISC² and serving on the advisory board of Sifarish. Closer to home, he has served as an evaluator of court interpreters, helping make sure justice is understood by the people it affects; he was invited to speak for Canada to new immigrants, sharing his own story as they build their lives here; and he continues to advocate for helping seniors live independently and with dignity.

Through QTSI, he brings all of it to the table, not as the person with every answer, but as someone who has learned a great deal and is eager to share it with the leaders and organizations willing to take this shift seriously.
